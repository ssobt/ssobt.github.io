02

From a patient cohort to a drug target

One project from start to finish. The short version:

  1. Some tumors are made of cells that all act alike. Others are a patchwork, and the patchwork ones are harder to treat.
  2. In over a thousand patient tumors, we looked for genes that come with more patchwork. 41 stood out.
  3. We turned 16 of them down in cancer cells. For two, RNF8 and MIS18A, the cells became more alike.
  4. Lowering either gene made cancer cells easier to kill with chemotherapy. In patients, high levels predict shorter survival.

Both figures below are interactive and computed from the real data.

Step 01 · Patient data

Which genes come with more varied tumors?

Most cancer studies ask which genes are higher in aggressive tumors. We asked something different: which genes, when they are high, come with more variability across the whole transcriptome?

We focused on chromatin organizers, the proteins that package DNA and control which genes can be switched on. For each one, we split TCGA breast cancer patients into those with the highest and lowest levels and compared how varied each group was. 41 genes stood out.

Reading Figure 1. Each dot is one patient's tumor. Orange tumors have high levels of the selected gene, blue tumors low. If the gene drives variability, the orange cloud spreads wider than the blue. Try RNF8 and MIS18A, then compare with the two control genes.

Methods detail

Groups were the top and bottom expression quartiles. Variability was genome-wide transcript coefficient of variation, compared against an empirical null built from 50 random patient groupings; the 41 candidates cleared z > 2.5 under both quartile and quintile splits. The figure projects patients into PC space and measures how far each sits from its own group's centroid.

    Why we didn't trust P-values alone. With groups this large, almost any gene looks significant: 84% of randomly chosen genes reach P < 0.05 on this metric. So each candidate was ranked against 200 random genes instead. ADORA2B shows why this matters. Its P = 2.7 × 10⁻⁵ looks impressive, but it beats only 74% of random genes. RNF8 and MIS18A beat 94% and 96%.

    We also ruled out the obvious alternative explanations: changes in DNA copy number, how pure the tumor sample was, and shifts in average expression.

    Step 02 · Wet-lab collaboration

    Test the candidates in living cells

    A pattern in patient data is a lead, not proof. Working with the bench side of the lab, we took 16 of the 41 candidates into a CRISPR screen in breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231). Each gene was turned down (CRISPRi) or up (CRISPRa), and every cell's transcriptome was read out (Perturb-seq).

    Two genes behaved consistently in both directions: RNF8, which helps repair DNA damage, and MIS18A, part of the machinery that marks centromeres, the anchor points chromosomes use during cell division. Neither had been linked to tumor variability before.

    Step 03 · Single-cell validation

    Watch the effect cell by cell

    Patient data compares tumors with each other. The real claim is about cells within a tumor, so we looked at single cells.

    Turning down either gene made the cells more alike. Variability dropped to 0.92× (MIS18A) and 0.93× (RNF8) of control cells, with about two-thirds of genes becoming less variable. Average expression didn't change, and neither did how fast the cells were dividing. What changed was variability itself.

    Reading Figure 2. Each dot is one cell, colored by which gene was turned down. Use the CO (chromatin organizer) buttons to compare one knockdown with the non-targeting controls: the knockdown cells form a tighter cloud. Each group shows the same number of cells, so you are seeing a difference in spread, not in sample size.

    Methods detail

    Variability is genome-wide transcript CV on linear relative counts. Each perturbation is compared against a size-matched control sample, because dispersion estimates scale with cell number; non-targeting cells split against themselves land at 1.01×. 70% (MIS18A) and 66% (RNF8) of tested genes moved down.

    The figure shows 471 cells per group, the size of the smallest. This is not a hand-picked view: across 200 random equal-size draws, RNF8 and MIS18A each sit tighter than non-targeting in 80–88% of them, and MIS18A in 99.5% of draws in UMAP space.

    Per-gene mean expression ratio is 1.00 (r = 0.999), and cell-cycle composition is unchanged. In the full screen AURKB runs the other way at 1.62×, which the paper also flags: perturbing an essential mitotic regulator has effects far beyond the one being measured.

      Step 04 · Why it matters

      Show it changes how tumors respond

      Lowering either gene made cancer cells more sensitive to chemotherapy and, in mice, reduced their spread to the lung. Raising it did the opposite. The result held in a second, independently engineered cell line.

      It holds in patients too. In a separate breast cancer cohort (METABRIC), high levels of either gene predict shorter survival; for MIS18A the hazard is 75% higher (HR = 1.75). That makes these genes both a marker of worse outcome and a potential drug target.

      05

      About

      I started in a wet lab. Two years as a postbaccalaureate researcher in Astrid Haase's lab at the NIH, making PAR-CLIP libraries and editing Drosophila stem cells with CRISPR, taught me what sequencing data actually is before I ever wrote a pipeline to process it.

      I did my PhD in Hani Goodarzi's lab at UCSF, finishing in 2025. The thesis was about finding targetable transcriptional states in breast cancer — which in practice meant moving between pooled genetic screens, patient cohorts, chromatin accessibility, deep learning, and the occasional microscope, and building the software that tied them together.

      What I keep coming back to is heterogeneity: the fact that genetically identical cancer cells behave differently, that this variability is itself regulated, and that regulating it changes how tumors respond to treatment. Two of my first-author projects are about measuring it and finding what tunes it.

      I'm looking for computational biology roles where the analysis has to survive contact with real decisions — target selection, trial design, a platform other people depend on. I care about work being reproducible by someone who isn't me.
