02

From a patient cohort to a drug target

This is the actual path of the chromatin-organizer study — public patient data in, a therapeutic target out, with wet-lab collaboration in the middle. The two figures are live: hand-built against canvas, no plotting library. Try the negative controls.

Step 01 · Public patient data

Ask 1,200 tumours which genes track with heterogeneity

Start with TCGA breast cancer RNA-seq and ask an unusual question of it. Not which genes are higher in aggressive tumours — that question has been asked exhaustively — but which genes, when highly expressed, come with a more variable transcriptome across patients.

For every chromatin organizer, split the cohort into top and bottom expression quartiles and compare genome-wide transcript coefficient of variation between the groups, against an empirical null built from 50 random patient groupings. 41 candidates cleared a z > 2.5 cutoff under both quartile and quintile splits.

The figure below is how we visualized it: project patients into PC space and measure how far each sits from its own group's centroid.

Why the controls are the important part. Split a cohort by any gene and you will get two groups; some difference in spread is guaranteed. Switching the selector to ADORA2B or ARHGEF5 is what makes the result mean something — same cohort, same method, effect gone. We also ruled out the three boring explanations: copy-number change (CNA and expression variability were uncorrelated), tumour purity (ESTIMATE scores), and a shift in mean expression, which does not move at all.

Step 02 · Wet-lab collaboration

Put the 41 candidates through a functional screen

A correlation in patient data is a hypothesis, not a target. The 41 candidates went into an in vivo CRISPRi screen run with the bench side of the lab — the step where a computational shortlist either survives contact with biology or does not.

Two came through: RNF8, a DNA-damage-response ubiquitin ligase, and MIS18A, part of the centromeric histone deposition complex. Neither had been connected to transcriptional heterogeneity before.

Step 03 · Single-cell validation

Show the effect at the level it actually operates

Patient-level variability is between tumours. The claim was about variability within one. So: CRISPRi and CRISPRa against both genes in breast cancer cells, read out by scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq.

Perturbing either gene shifts transcriptomic variability and chromatin accessibility dispersion together — without changing mean expression or copy number. That dissociation is the whole argument: these genes tune how noisy a population is, not how much it expresses.

    Step 04 · Actionable

    Make it matter for treatment

    A regulator of noise is only interesting if turning it changes an outcome. Knocking down either gene sensitized cells to chemotherapy and reduced lung metastatic burden in vivo; overexpressing it conferred resistance and promoted progression. The effect reproduced in a second, independently engineered cell line.

    And it carries to patients: in METABRIC, higher expression of either gene predicts shorter survival — MIS18A at HR = 1.75. Both a prognostic marker and a therapeutic handle, from a question first asked of a public cohort.

    05

    About

    I started in a wet lab. Two years as a postbaccalaureate researcher in Astrid Haase's lab at the NIH, making PAR-CLIP libraries and editing Drosophila stem cells with CRISPR, taught me what sequencing data actually is before I ever wrote a pipeline to process it.

    I did my PhD in Hani Goodarzi's lab at UCSF, finishing in 2025. The thesis was about finding targetable transcriptional states in breast cancer — which in practice meant moving between pooled genetic screens, patient cohorts, chromatin accessibility, deep learning, and the occasional microscope, and building the software that tied them together.

    What I keep coming back to is heterogeneity: the fact that genetically identical cancer cells behave differently, that this variability is itself regulated, and that regulating it changes how tumours respond to treatment. Two of my first-author projects are about measuring it and finding what tunes it.

    I'm looking for computational biology roles where the analysis has to survive contact with real decisions — target selection, trial design, a platform other people depend on. I care about work being reproducible by someone who isn't me.
