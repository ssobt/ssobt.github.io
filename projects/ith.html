bioRxiv 2026 · co-first author

Chromatin organizers as tuners of intratumoral heterogeneity

Genetically identical cancer cells do not behave identically. We asked what controls how much they differ — and found chromatin organizers that set the level of transcriptional noise, with consequences for how tumours respond to chemotherapy.

  • scATAC-seq
  • scRNA-seq
  • CRISPRi / CRISPRa
  • TCGA
  • survival analysis
  • ArchR

The question

Intratumoral heterogeneity — the variability between cancer cells within a single tumour — is a well-established predictor of poor outcome. The usual reading is that heterogeneity is a consequence: of mutation, of clonal evolution, of microenvironment.

We wanted to test a different premise. If heterogeneity is partly regulated — if specific genes set how noisy a population's transcriptome is, independent of the population's mean expression — then those genes are targets. Turning the noise down could make a tumour behave more uniformly, and a more uniform tumour is an easier one to treat.

Approach

The hard part is measurement. Most differential-expression machinery is built to compare means; we needed to compare dispersion, and to do it consistently across three assays that fail in different ways.

So I built the measurement layer first, as txnheterogeneity: transcriptome-wide coefficient of variation for bulk and single-cell RNA-seq, dispersion in principal-component space as a complementary bulk metric, and — wrapping ArchR for peak calling — the same comparison over open chromatin in scATAC-seq. Significance comes from sampled background distributions rather than a parametric assumption, because gene-level CV is not well behaved.

With that in place the study ran on three fronts:

  • Patient data. Bulk RNA-seq from hundreds of TCGA breast cancer patients, stratified by expression of candidate regulators, asking which candidates separate high- from low-heterogeneity tumours and whether that split tracks with survival.
  • Perturbation. CRISPRi and CRISPRa against the candidates in breast cancer lines, read out by scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq — so we could see whether knocking a gene down or up moved heterogeneity in the direction the patient data predicted, and whether the chromatin and transcriptional signals agreed.
  • Phenotype. Whether shifting heterogeneity changed the response to chemotherapy, which is the point of the exercise.

What we found

RNF8 and MIS18A — both chromatin organizers, neither previously characterized this way — behave as tuners of transcriptional heterogeneity. Perturbing them changes the global variability of open chromatin in scATAC-seq, not just expression variability downstream, which places the effect at the chromatin layer rather than in transcriptional output alone.

In TCGA, the heterogeneity axis these genes define is associated with worse survival outcomes — the patient signal and the cell-line perturbation point the same way.

Separately, both RNF8/MIS18A and the cGAS/STING pathway emerged as targets for improving combinatorial chemotherapy response — RNF8 and MIS18A in combination with 5-FU and cyclophosphamide, cGAS/STING with gemcitabine and doxorubicin.

The part I'd defend hardest: that the measurement is real and not an artifact of depth. Dispersion metrics are notoriously sensitive to sequencing depth, cell number, and cell-cycle composition — a "more heterogeneous" sample is very often just a differently-covered one. Most of the work in txnheterogeneity is controls: sampled background distributions, gene-retention thresholds, explicit cell-cycle breakdowns, and matched cell counts per condition.

What I'd do next

  • Mechanism. We show RNF8 and MIS18A tune heterogeneity; we do not fully show how. RNF8's role in the DNA-damage response and MIS18A's at the centromere suggest distinct routes, and separating them would need targeted domain mutants rather than whole-gene knockdown.
  • Beyond cell lines. The perturbation work is in lines. Organoids or PDX would test whether the same tuning survives a realistic microenvironment.
  • Prospective, not retrospective. The TCGA association is retrospective. The real test is whether a heterogeneity score measured before treatment predicts response — that needs a cohort with pre-treatment sequencing and recorded outcomes.
  • Make the metric portable. Right now the method is an R package that assumes you have the objects in memory. A Nextflow wrapper taking FASTQs to a heterogeneity report would make it usable by people who are not me.
