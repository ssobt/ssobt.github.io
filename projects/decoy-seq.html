Decoy-seq: screening the un-screenable

Small non-coding RNAs resist the tools that made CRISPR screens routine. Decoy-seq sequesters them instead of cutting them — which turns an awkward, one-at-a-time class of regulators into something you can screen in a pool.

  • Perturb-seq
  • miRNA · tRNA fragments
  • pooled screens
  • PyTorch VAE
  • MDA-MB-231

The question

Regulatory small non-coding RNAs — microRNAs, and the tRNA-derived fragments that have increasingly turned up as regulators rather than debris — are hard to screen. They are short, they come in families with near-identical sequences, many are processed from loci that encode other things, and knocking out the locus often destroys something you did not mean to touch. The result is a field that has mostly proceeded one molecule at a time.

Meanwhile tRNA fragments in particular kept appearing in breast cancer as biomarkers — correlates of disease state with no established function. That is usually a sign the tools are the limiting factor, not the biology.

Approach

Rather than destroying the RNA, Decoy-seq removes it from circulation. Tough-decoy constructs present high-affinity binding sites that soak up the target small RNA, inhibiting it without touching its locus. Because the decoy is an expressed, barcoded construct, it behaves like any other pooled-screen element — which makes the whole class amenable to a Perturb-seq readout: pool the decoy library, transduce, and sequence single cells so each cell reports both which inhibitor it received and its full transcriptional response.

I worked on the computational side: taking the single-cell readout from a library inhibiting miRNAs and tRNA fragments in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells, and working out what each perturbation actually did.

Two pieces of that were method work rather than analysis:

  • A variational autoencoder, built in PyTorch, for dimensionality reduction across the perturbation library. A VAE's latent space is continuous and probabilistic, which suits a screen where most perturbations do almost nothing and the interesting ones shift cells slightly along shared axes — the regime where discrete clustering tends to either over-split or wash the signal out.
  • VariPath, an R package for the pathway question. Raw principal components of a Perturb-seq matrix are dense and hard to read biologically. Applying a varimax rotation to the component space concentrates loadings onto fewer genes, which makes each component far more interpretable when handed to enrichment — a better signal-to-noise ratio for perturbation-to-pathway association than the comparable approaches we tested.

Why the decoy design matters analytically: because inhibition is competitive rather than destructive, effect sizes are graded rather than binary. That is harder to analyse than a clean knockout — there is no on/off to threshold — but it is also closer to the biology, since these regulators act by degree in the first place. It is the main reason the analysis leans on continuous latent representations instead of differential-expression calls per guide.

What we found

Decoy-seq works as a screening platform: the pooled decoy library plus a single-cell readout recovers interpretable transcriptional responses across a class of regulators that previously had to be studied individually.

The result I find most interesting is about the tRNA fragments. Several that had been treated purely as breast cancer biomarkers turned out to have functional transcriptional consequences when inhibited — they are not just correlates of disease state, they are doing something. That reframes a set of molecules from readouts into candidate nodes.

What I'd do next

  • Specificity controls at scale. Decoys can plausibly sequester related sequences within a family. Establishing per-decoy specificity systematically — rather than per-candidate — is the main thing standing between this and routine use.
  • Beyond one cell line. Everything here is MDA-MB-231. Whether the tRNA-fragment functions are context-dependent is an open and fairly important question.
  • Combinatorial decoys. These regulators act redundantly by design; single inhibition likely understates their contribution. Pairwise decoys are a natural extension the pooled format already supports.
  • Foundation-model priors. I have since fine-tuned Geneformer for perturbation inference on in-house data. Using a pre-trained single-cell model to prioritise which perturbations are worth deep characterisation would make the next screen cheaper.
